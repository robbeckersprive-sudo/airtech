Dust explosion risk:
what to look for with ATEX

A dust explosion needs five things at once: combustible dust, oxygen, an ignition source, a dust cloud and an enclosed space. Filters, cyclones, silos and conveying lines often bring those five together. That is why explosion safety belongs in the design, not as an afterthought.

Five conditions for a dust explosion

A fire needs fuel, oxygen and an ignition source. A dust explosion adds two more conditions: the dust is suspended in the air as a cloud, and the space is enclosed, so pressure builds up. Together they form the explosion pentagon. Remove one side and no explosion follows.

Finer dust is more dangerous. Small particles have a large surface area, ignite more easily and burn faster. Dry dust also stays airborne longer. A product that seems harmless as granules can be explosive as a fine fraction in a filter. So look at the dust where it is generated, not just at the product.

A first, small explosion can stir up settled dust. That new cloud can cause a much more severe secondary explosion. Good housekeeping is therefore also part of explosion safety.

This article helps you ask the right questions. It does not replace a risk assessment: you carry that out for your own situation, with an expert where needed.

  • Prevent: no cloud, no ignition
  • Limit: pressure, flame and propagation

Two directives, two roles

ATEX consists of two European directives. Directive 2014/34/EU, also known as ATEX 114, covers equipment and protective systems intended for use in potentially explosive atmospheres. That directive is aimed at the manufacturer. Directive 1999/92/EC, also known as ATEX 153, covers the safety and health of workers. That directive is aimed at the employer.

In the Netherlands, ATEX 153 is implemented in the Dutch Working Conditions Decree (Arbobesluit), in the section on explosive atmospheres (Articles 3.5a to 3.5f). The employer records the risks in an explosion protection document (Article 3.5c) and classifies areas with an explosion risk into zones (Article 3.5d). For our collaboration this means: you provide the zone classification and the risk assessment, and we match the design of the installation to them.

Zones 20, 21 and 22: where is the cloud?

Directive 1999/92/EC classifies places with combustible dust into three zones. Zone 20 is a place in which an explosive atmosphere in the form of a cloud of combustible dust in air is present continuously, or for long periods or frequently. Zone 21 is a place in which such an atmosphere is likely to occur in normal operation occasionally. Zone 22 is a place in which it is not likely to occur in normal operation but, if it does occur, will persist for a short period only.

The inside of a filter or silo is often zone 20, the area around a tipping point often zone 21 or 22. These are examples, not a rule. The zone classification is determined for each situation, with NEN-EN-IEC 60079-10-2 as the standard for classifying areas with combustible dust.

Dust data drives the design

Which measures are needed depends on the dust. The key characteristics are the Kst value (how fast the pressure rises in an explosion), the maximum explosion pressure Pmax, the minimum ignition energy (MIE), the minimum ignition temperature of a dust cloud and of a dust layer, and the lower explosion limit. Particle size and moisture content affect all of these values.

This data is often not included in a safety data sheet. Laboratory testing of a representative sample gives the most reliable values. A reference database such as GESTIS-STAUB-EX from the IFA gives a first indication. The IFA itself points out that dusts that appear similar can have very different values. So do not use such values as a design basis.

Preventing ignition

The first line is prevention: no dust cloud where none is needed, and no ignition source where a cloud can occur. Keep the installation dust-tight and clean up settled dust. Earth all conductive parts of pipework and components and connect them to each other (equipotential bonding), so that static charge can dissipate.

Static charge also needs attention in the filter, with a conductive or antistatic filter medium, and with big bags. A type C big bag is conductive and must be earthed during filling and discharging. Type D is made of insulating material and limits the charge without earthing. Rotating parts, such as rotary valves and fans, can produce mechanical sparks or hot surfaces. Foreign bodies in the product, such as bolts or stones, are caught beforehand with magnets and sieves.

Limiting the consequences

If ignition cannot be ruled out, constructional measures limit the consequences. With explosion venting, an explosion vent panel opens so that pressure and flame escape in a safe direction (EN 14491). If the equipment is indoors, flameless venting keeps the flame and hot dust inside the equipment (EN 16009). Explosion suppression detects an explosion at an early stage and extinguishes it before the pressure rises high (EN 14373). An explosion-pressure-resistant design is built to withstand the explosion pressure (EN 14460).

An explosion must not propagate through pipework to other parts of the installation. Explosion isolation (EN 15089) prevents this, for example with non-return valves, fast-acting valves or a rotary valve. A rotary valve only counts as isolation in a version that has been tested for that purpose. In wood processing, spark detection with extinguishing in the pipework is a common principle: a spark is extinguished before it reaches the filter.

What this means for each component

Explosion safety is a property of the whole installation, not of a single component. The filter is often the place with the highest dust concentration and is therefore usually given venting, suppression or a pressure-resistant design. A cyclone separates the coarse dust, but is itself also an enclosed space with a dust cloud. A rotary valve under a filter or cyclone can provide isolation, provided it has been tested. A fan on the clean side of the filter handles much less dust than a fan on the dust side.

Pipework must be earthed and bonded, and is the route along which an explosion can propagate. A silo with a vent filter is a classic place for a dust cloud during pneumatic filling. At a big bag station, tipping, static charge and the type of big bag all play a role. In pneumatic conveying the situation differs per system: with vacuum conveying, dust stays inside the line in the event of a leak; with pressure conveying, it escapes. Conveying with nitrogen or another inert gas keeps the oxygen content so low that an explosion cannot occur; this is a principle that is designed for each application.

From insight to your situation

Recognise your situation and see which information helps determine the next step.

An indoor wood dust filter is being extended

Look again at where the pressure can go. A vent panel that discharges into the hall is not an option. Flameless venting, suppression or moving the filter outdoors are alternatives. More extraction points also mean a new air balance.

A flour or sugar silo is filled pneumatically

Include the filling cycle. During filling there is a dust cloud in the silo. Vent filter, earthing, filling rate and silo venting should be assessed together.

An existing installation is given additional extraction points

Update the explosion protection document. New extraction points change the airflow, the dust load on the filter and sometimes the zones. So review the explosion protection document and the zone classification before the extension goes into operation.

What do you need for initial advice?

  • ProductProduct name and safety data sheet.
  • Dust dataKst, Pmax, St class, MIE and minimum ignition temperature, where known.
  • Zone classificationThe zone classification or the explosion protection document.
  • LayoutIndoors or outdoors, distance to workstations and free space for venting.
  • Existing measuresVent panels, isolation, earthing or spark detection already in place.
  • Drawing or photosOf the installation and its surroundings.

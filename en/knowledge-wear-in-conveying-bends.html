Why does a
conveying bend wear?

Replacing the same bend again and again costs production time. Where the wall becomes thin, how many tonnes passed through it and which material you convey together tell you which change stands a chance.

To the comparisonWhere do you start?

Short answer

Map the location
Note the flow direction, the position of the bend and the wear pattern. Include the straight section immediately after the bend.
Compare per tonne
Record how much product the bend has handled between two inspections. With varying production, that says more than the number of months alone.

Why in the bend?

The conveying air follows the curve of the pipe. A particle entering the bend keeps moving some way in its original direction because of its inertia. Where particles hit or slide along the wall, material can be removed from the wall. The particles themselves can also break.

The traces differ per installation. Hardness and shape of the particles play a part, as do their velocity and the angle at which they hit the wall. Two identical bends in the same line can therefore have a different service life. That difference is a useful starting point for investigation.

  • Assessing the existing line
  • Testing a change

What does the worn spot tell you?

Mark on a drawing which bend was replaced. Draw the flow direction and indicate where the wall was thinnest. Is the spot always on the outside of the bend or rather in the outlet? Lay photos of removed parts side by side in the same orientation.

Check whether the service life changed after a recipe change, a higher production rate or a change to the air supply. In a pressure conveying line with a constant diameter, the air can accelerate further along as the pressure drops. So include the position relative to the end of the line as well.

Which change fits the pattern?

A wear-resistant lining changes the material the particles hit. A bend with a vortex chamber redirects the product flow in a different way. With product changes it also matters whether material is left behind. Ask how the chosen design can be emptied and inspected.

If one bend consistently suits the application poorly, the route is also worth a look. Sometimes a change of direction can be eliminated. Have a proposal for a different air velocity checked against the complete conveying route. For stable conveying, the material must keep moving even under the least favourable operating conditions.

From wear pattern to investigation

Use the observation to measure in a targeted way and to compare an alternative for the same task.

From insight to your situation

Recognise your situation and see which information helps determine the next step.

We now replace the bend every quarter

Work back to production. A quarter with double production is a different load. Record tonnage and product mix per bend, so that a trial with a different wall material can be assessed fairly.

The outer wall lasts longer, but the granules break

Take a product sample at two points. Compare the fine fraction at the inlet and at the receiver from the same batch. In a next trial you can then assess both service life and product quality.

A second product is added to the same line

Include product residues in the choice. A bend with a chamber calls for a different assessment with product changes than a smooth, continuous bend. Discuss how you check after emptying that the line is clean enough for the next product.

What do you need for initial advice?

  • RouteSketch with flow direction, pipe diameter, bend radius and location of the wall loss.
  • LoadProduct types, tonnage since installation, air flow and pressure in normal operation.
  • OutcomeRequired service life, acceptable product breakage and requirements for product changes.
